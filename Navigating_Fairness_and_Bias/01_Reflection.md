Fairness and bias in data‑driven predictive models constitute a central challenge in contemporary data science, particularly as such models are increasingly used to inform decisions that materially affect individuals and social groups. Because predictive models are learned from historical data, they inevitably reflect the structural inequalities, measurement limitations, and institutional practices embedded in those data. When deployed in high‑stakes contexts, these inherited biases can translate into systematic and unequal harms. A foundational consideration in fair model development is the role of protected attributes. In the United States, characteristics such as race, gender, age, religion, and national origin are legally restricted in many decision‑making domains, including employment and lending. The permissibility of using such attributes depends on both the attribute itself and the application area, with legal requirements varying across federal and state jurisdictions. Consequently, model builders must exercise due diligence to ensure that prohibited attributes are not directly or indirectly incorporated into predictive systems in ways that violate legal or ethical norms.

The harms arising from biased models are commonly categorized as allocative or representational. Allocative harm occurs when algorithmic decisions unfairly deny or grant access to scarce resources such as jobs, loans, housing, education, or social benefits. Representational harm arises when model outputs distort, diminish, or mischaracterize individuals or groups, reinforcing stereotypes or enabling increased surveillance and loss of liberty. While predictive error is unavoidable, the ethical concern lies in the disproportionate distribution of error across groups, particularly when those errors systematically disadvantage already marginalized populations.

One major source of unequal error is representation bias, which occurs when training datasets underrepresent certain groups while overrepresenting others. This imbalance often stems from historical discrimination, unequal access to services, affordability constraints, or reliance on pre‑existing datasets assembled for convenience rather than equity. As a result, models trained on such data have limited exposure to underrepresented populations and perform less reliably when applied to them. Diagnosing representation bias requires disaggregating model performance metrics across relevant subgroups and identifying disparities in error rates, rather than relying solely on aggregate accuracy.
Measurement bias represents a distinct and equally consequential challenge. It arises when proxy variables are used in place of true outcomes because the latter are difficult or costly to observe. These proxies are frequently derived from human judgments or institutional records, such as performance ratings, diagnoses, expenditures, or arrest data, all of which may encode systematic biases. In such cases, increasing the quantity of data does not resolve the underlying problem, as the labels themselves are distorted. Effective mitigation therefore depends on careful scrutiny of label validity, collaboration with domain experts, and, when necessary, investment in collecting more accurate outcome measures.

Mitigation strategies for bias operate at multiple stages of the modeling process. These include targeted data collection to improve representation, algorithmic modifications designed to promote fairness, and post‑processing techniques that adjust model outputs according to explicit fairness criteria. One commonly used criterion is equal opportunity, operationalized by aligning true positive rates across groups so that qualified individuals have comparable chances of receiving positive outcomes. However, improving fairness with respect to one metric may exacerbate disparities in others, underscoring the need for informed trade‑offs grounded in domain knowledge. Overall, fairness in predictive modeling requires sustained, intentional engagement with data quality, labeling practices, evaluation metrics, and downstream impacts. Bias is not a peripheral concern but a core technical and ethical issue that must be addressed throughout the model development lifecycle.
